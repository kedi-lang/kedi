# Cities Information in Turkey


## İstanbul

### History
İstanbul is a city with a rich history, dating back over 2,500 years. Originally founded as Byzantium in the 7th century BCE, it became the capital of the Roman Empire as Constantinople in 330 CE. In 1453, the Ottoman Empire conquered the city, renaming it İstanbul, which marked the beginning of its golden age as a center of trade, culture, and Islamic art. Landmarks like the Hagia Sophia and the Topkapi Palace reflect its layered past. The city played a pivotal role in connecting Europe and Asia, blending Byzantine, Ottoman, and Mediterranean influences into its historical identity.

### Economy
İstanbul is Turkey’s economic heart, contributing over 30% of the country’s GDP. It is a global financial hub, hosting the Istanbul Stock Exchange and thousands of multinational corporations. Its strategic location on the Bosporus Strait makes it a critical gateway for maritime trade, facilitating 25% of the world’s maritime cargo. Key industries include banking, energy, tourism, and manufacturing, particularly in sectors like automotive and textiles. The city’s bustling port, Istanbul Port, and transportation networks further solidify its role as a commercial nexus between Europe and Asia.

### Cuisine
İstanbul’s cuisine is a vibrant tapestry of flavors reflecting its diverse heritage. Signature dishes include succulent kebabs, rich stews like *kazandibi*, and delicate pastries such as *baklava* and *börek*. Street food is iconic: *simit* (sesame bread rings), *döner kebab*, and *balık ekmek* (fish and bread on the Bosporus). The city’s meyhane culture offers meze (small plates) to accompany local *rakı* drinks. Its coastal location brings an array of seafood dishes, while markets like the famed Grand Bazaar showcase spices, olives, and traditional sweets. Turkish tea and coffee culture further highlight the city’s culinary warmth and hospitality.


## Ankara

### History
Ankara's history dates back to ancient Hatti civilizations and the Hittite Empire. It later became the Roman city of Ancyra and Byzantine ANkyra. In 1923, it was chosen as the new capital of the Republic of Turkey, replacing Istanbul, marking its role as the heart of the modern Turkish state.

### Economy
Ankara serves as Turkey's political, cultural, and economic hub. Its economy thrives in sectors like automotive, electronics, textiles, and defense industries, supported by government institutions and modern infrastructure.

### Cuisine
Ankara's cuisine features traditional Turkish dishes such as mantı (spiced meat dumplings) and testi kebabı (slow-cooked clay pot meat), reflecting regional flavors with a balance of spices and grilled meats. It also showcases desserts like baklava and lokum (Turkish delight).


## İzmir

### History
İzmir (formerly Smyrna) has a rich history spanning ancient Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman rule. It was a cultural and commercial center during the Roman era and became a focal point in the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922), which led to a major population exchange between Greece and Turkey in 1923. This reshaped İzmir into a cosmopolitan city with diverse cultural influences, reflected in its architecture, languages, and traditions.

### Economy
İzmir's economy is driven by its role as Turkey’s third-largest city and a major port. It serves as a hub for industrial production, textile manufacturing, and agriculture, with the port facilitating Mediterranean trade. The economy benefits from infrastructure projects, transportation networks, and tech innovation centers like the İzmir Technopark. Tourism and historical industries, such as olive oil production, further contribute to its economic diversity.

### Cuisine
İzmir's cuisine is a vibrant blend of Turkish and Mediterranean flavors. The city is renowned for its fresh seafood, including dishes like İzmir mackerel and meze platters featuring eggplant, feta cheese, and grilled vegetables. Traditional pastries such as böreks and baklavalı ekmek (bread layered with phyllo and nuts) are local specialties. The region’s tea culture, particularly the production of black tea, is also a significant culinary heritage.


## Bursa

### History
Bursa was a pivotal center of the early Ottoman Empire, capturing it in the 13th century. It served as the empire’s first capital and remains home to historical landmarks like the Green Mosque (Yeşil Cami) and the Grand Mosque (Ulu Cami). The city also hosts thermals springs and the Uludağ National Park, reflecting its long-standing cultural and natural significance.

### Economy
Bursa is a major industrial and commercial hub in Turkey, known for its textile, automotive parts, and machinery industries. It serves as a critical trade route to Europe via the Marmaray rail system and houses the Bursa Exporters Assembly, driving regional economic growth and diversification.

### Cuisine
Bursa is renowned for its culinary traditions, particularly its yogurt-based dishes, mantı (small meat-stuffed dumplings), and börek. It is also famous for its unique take on döner kebab and sweet treats like Bursa baklava, influenced by the region's agricultural resources and Ottoman heritage.


## Antalya

### History
Antalya has a rich history dating back to Hellenistic and Roman times. The region became a major Byzantine port before falling under Seljuk and later Ottoman rule. The 13th-century Antalya Castle and the ruins of Perge and Olympos reflect its layered past as a center of trade, religion, and imperial power, blending Greek, Roman, and Islamic influences into its cultural identity.

### Economy
Antalya's economy is primarily driven by tourism, bolstered by its sunny climate, historical sites, and coastline. Key industries include hospitality, agriculture (citrus fruits, olives, and cotton), and manufacturing. Real estate and international investments have surged in recent decades, while markets like Kaleiç and Konakville showcase thriving commerce and artisanal goods.

### Cuisine
Antalya's cuisine is a vibrant part of its cultural heritage, featuring traditional Turkish dishes with a Mediterranean twist. Dishes like *kumru* (grilled lamb), *mantı* (stuffed dumplings), and *balık ekmek* (fish sandwiches) highlight the region's coastal influence. Fresh vegetables, seafood, and meze-style small plates are staples, with local specialties such as *ırmık* (buckwheat dishes) reflecting the area's agrarian roots.


## Gaziantep

### History
With 7,000 years of history as 'Azaz,' Gaziantep was a Silk Road crossroads controlled by Hittites, Assyrians, and Romans. The Seljuks walled the city in the 12th century before Saladin's conquest in 1185. Ottoman Sultan Selim I incorporated it in 1516, leaving monumental architecture like Hevsel Gardens (a UNESCO site). Its strategic position between Anatolia and Mesopotamia fostered cultural synthesis evident in the 16th-century Korkut大门 and 19th-century Hanımım Bedesten market's hybrid Turkish-Arab architectural style.

### Economy
The economy thrives on a blend of historical craftsmanship and modern industry. Textile manufacturing, metalworking for 'gazpacho' spoons and jewelry, and leather production from Ottoman-era workshops underpin traditional sectors. Contemporary growth drivers include automotive parts, halal food exports, and a burgeoning tech sector supported by Gaziantep University. Tourism also plays a key role, with UNESCO-listed citadels and historic caravanserais attracting visitors.

### Cuisine
Gaziantep is renowned for its rich culinary heritage, home to iconic dishes like gaziantep baklava (a layered pastry filled with pistachios and rose water) and spicy gazpacho-like salads made with melon, yogurt, mint, and sumac. Local markets overflow with unique meze such as hilbeh (fermented fava bean paste) and sığır ekşisi (sour cow meat), showcasing the region’s ancient trade-routed influences through its use of pomegranate molasses, sumac, and Armenian cucumbers. The Old City’s spice bazaars remain central to preserving this gastronomic legacy.


## Adana

### History
Adana has a rich history dating back to ancient times, with roots in the Hittite and Assyrian empires (circa 1500 BCE). It flourished as a key city in the Hellenistic and Roman periods, becoming a center for trade and culture. The city was known as Adandus in Roman times and later became a vital hub in the Byzantine and Ottoman Empires. Modern Adana has expanded into a bustling urban center while preserving its historical legacy through sites like the Covered Market (Kapalı Çarşı) and the Anıtkabir-like Atatürk Monument.

### Economy
Adana's economy thrives on agriculture, industry, and commerce. It is a major production center for cotton, citrus fruits, sugar beets, and sunflowers. The city's industrial zones, such as Kent and Toros, focus on textiles, machinery, and food processing. Adana also hosts a significant logistics and trade hub, supported by its port on the Seyhan River and strategic location along Turkey's Southern Corridor. Tourism related to historical sites like the Hadrian's Gate and the 15 August Martyrs' Museum further contributes to the economy.

### Cuisine
Adana's cuisine is renowned for its spicy and flavorful dishes. The city is famous for Adana kebab, a minced meat kebab spiced with red pepper and sumac, typically grilled over charcoal. Other local specialties include sucuk (a cured sausage), lahmacun (a thin, crispy flatbread topped with spiced meat and vegetables), and lentil soup. The region's agricultural abundance also provides fresh produce, olive oil, and dairy products, which are integral to its meze culture and traditional dishes.


## Konya

### History
Konya’s history spans millennia, from its ancient roots as **Conaeum** under the Hittites to its golden age as the capital of the **Sultanate of Rum** (12-13th centuries). The Seljuk Sultan **Kilij Arslan I** established a cultural renaissance here, exemplified by landmarks like the **Great Mosque of Konya** and the **Seljuk Palaces**. It became a sanctuary for mysticism under the Mevlevi Order, founded by **Mevlana Jalaluddin Rumi**, whose spiritual legacy continues to attract pilgrims and scholars worldwide.

### Economy
Konya has historically thrived on agriculture, particularly wheat, cotton, and livestock, but now serves as a modern industrial and commercial hub. Key sectors include automotive manufacturing (with major companies like Škoda Auto Turkey and Ford Otosan), food processing, and textiles. Its strategic location along highways and railways enhances trade connectivity to both Eastern and Western Turkey. In recent years, IT and R&D sectors have also begun to grow, positioning Konya as an emerging tech center.

### Cuisine
Konya's cuisine is renowned for dishes such as **manti** (spiced meat dumplings), **kelle paça** (slow-cooked lamb head and feet), and **mercimek köftesi** (lentil and bulgur köfte). Sweet treats like **halva** and **gözleme** reflect the region’s Ottoman influences, while street food staples like **sığara böreği** (tobacco leaf pastries) showcase local ingenuity. The cuisine blends Central Anatolian simplicity with rich flavors passed down through generations.


## Mersin

### History
Mersin's history dates back to ancient Cilicia, with the notable city of Tarsus, birthplace of St. Paul. It was home to the Hittite, Persian, Roman, and Byzantine empires. The Seljuk and Mongol periods left lasting cultural marks, and Mersin became a key Ottoman port. Modern Mersin emerged as a province in 1922, blending its historical roots with contemporary developments.

### Economy
Mersin's economy thrives on agriculture (citrus fruits, olives), fishing, and its strategic Port of Mersin, which is Turkey's largest cargo port. Industries include mining (coal, copper), energy production, and manufacturing. The government's infrastructure investments and growing tourism sector further boost economic growth.

### Cuisine
Mersin's cuisine, particularly from the Cilician region, is renowned for dishes like keşkek (a wheat porridge with lentils and meat), cucumbers with yogurt (yogurt, garlic, and dill spread), and Mersin helva made from tahini. The coastal location contributes to a rich seafood tradition, and street foods like kavurma (roasted chestnuts) are also popular.


## Samsun

### History
With roots tracing to ancient Greek and Roman settlements, Samsun became a vital Ottoman port. It gained historical significance in 1919 as the site where Mustafa Kemal Atatürk launched the Turkish War of Independence. The city's landmarks include the 18th-century Nene Hatun Bridge, Samsun Fortress remnants, and the Atatürk House Museum, preserving its revolutionary legacy.

### Economy
Samsun's economy thrives on its strategic Black Sea port, a key trade hub for grain, agricultural goods, and Eastern European transit. The region's fertile plains support sunflower, hazelnut, and cereal farming, while industrial zones focus on food processing, machinery, and construction materials, cementing its economic dominance in Turkey’s Black Sea region.

### Cuisine
Samsun's cuisine is defined by its Black Sea roots, featuring dishes like hamsi (anchovy), mantı (spiced meat dumplings), and erkek cıtır (a soft, tangy cheese). Fermented black rice (şalgam), local yogurt-based sauces, and honey-roasted chestnuts highlight the region’s agricultural abundance, with nearby vineyards and olive groves adding to its food culture.


## Trabzon

### History
With roots in ancient Greek colonies, Trabzon became a Byzantine stronghold. The Empire of Trebizond, established in 1204 by the Komnenos dynasty, made it a cultural hub until Ottoman absorption in 1461. Its strategic port fueled trade, blending Persian, Greek, and Ottoman influences that shape its heritage today.

### Economy
Trabzon's economy thrives on agriculture (tea, corn, and hazelnuts), fishing, and forestry, supported by its Black Sea port. Tourism drives growth via historical sites like Hagia Sophia Church and natural attractions such as Uzungöl. Industry includes paper production from regional forests and food processing.

### Cuisine
Trabzon's cuisine is renowned for its Black Sea specialties, including hamsi (anchovies) prepared in traditional fish soup, grilled fish with cheese, and pastırma (spiced corned beef). Local dishes like örek (a cornbread) and lutek (stuffed grape leaves) reflect the region's agricultural abundance, while tea culture is deeply ingrained, often paired with sweets like Turkish Delight.


## Diyarbakır

### History
Diyarbakır, one of Turkey’s oldest continuously inhabited cities, has a history spanning 5,000 years. Known as Amida in antiquity, it was a strategic city in the Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman empires. The iconic Diyarbakır Fortress and Hevsel Gardens (UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2019) reflect its ancient roots and role as a cultural crossroads between Anatolia, Mesopotamia, and the Mediterranean.

### Economy
The economy is driven by agriculture (cotton, pomegranates, and apricots) and handicrafts like carpets and metalwork. Modern industries include food processing and construction materials. Its location at the Turkey-Iran-Iraq junction makes it a key trade hub, with vibrant markets such as Surp Giragos Church Bazaar reflecting its commercial heritage.

### Cuisine
Signature dishes include **keşk** (fermented wheat porridge with yogurt), **çöptürkü** (crispy herb fritters), and **şehnî** (sweet pomegranate paste desserts). The city is renowned for its pide (flatbread) and lamb-based kebabs. Regional specialties like *dolma* (stuffed vegetables) and *lahana ekmek* (cabbage rolls) highlight the blend of Turkish, Kurdish, and Mesopotamian culinary traditions.


## Erzurum

### History
Erzurum, one of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, was founded by the Urartians and later ruled by Medes, Persians, Romans, and Ottomans. It became a provincial capital in 1608 under the Ottomans, preserving architecture like the Artuklu Fountain and the Armenian Apostolic Church, which attest to its crossroads of civilizations.

### Economy
Erzurum's economy thrives on agriculture, livestock farming, and mining (copper, coal, and limestone). It's a hub for dairy and textile production, supported by its fertile lands and seasonal pastures. Tourism also grows due to natural attractions like the Van Lake and historical sites.

### Cuisine
Erzurum's cuisine is renowned for its hearty dishes like mantı (dumplings with garlic yogurt), kuzu testi (slow-cooked lamb in terracotta), and Erzurum helvası (a walnut-honey bar). The region emphasizes farm-fresh ingredients, dairy products, and traditional preservation methods, reflecting its nomadic and agricultural heritage.


## Van

### History
Van has a rich history dating back to ancient times as part of the Van Kingdom and later the Hittite Empire. It was a significant center under the Urartu civilization, evident in its archaeological sites like the Van Castle. During the Middle Ages, the region saw Armenian and Kurdish rule before becoming part of the Ottoman Empire. The modern city of Van continues to preserve its historical legacy through ruins, museums, and cultural traditions.

### Economy
Van's economy is driven by agriculture, particularly the production of cherries, apricots, and grapes. The region is also known for its livestock farming and handicrafts, such as handwoven Van rugs. Tourism plays a growing role due to attractions like Lake Van, ancient historical sites, and the Van Castle, which draw visitors seeking cultural and natural enrichment.

### Cuisine
Van's cuisine is renowned for its unique flavors, including dishes like Van kebab, cherry-filled pastries, and apricot preserves. The region's agricultural abundance, particularly cherries and apricots, heavily influences its culinary traditions. Local specialties also include traditional Armenian and Kurdish dishes, reflecting the area's diverse cultural heritage.


## Kars

### History
Kars has been inhabited since the 2nd millennium BCE as a Hittite settlement, later becoming a key Urartian city under King Sarduri I. It was a significant stop on the Silk Road during Roman and Byzantine rule. The Seljuks built the current Kars Castle in the 9th century, and it remained under Ottoman control until 1920. Its strategic location has shaped its rich cultural heritage of mixed Armenian, Turkish, and Kurdish influences.

### Economy
Kars' economy relies on agriculture (apricots, grains), livestock farming, mining (antimony, copper), and tourism. The region's rugged terrain supports shepherding, while historical sites like Kars Castle and Ottoman-era churches attract visitors. Recent investments in infrastructure and renewable energy projects are gradually diversifying economic opportunities.

### Cuisine
Kars is renowned for its unique cuisine, featuring dishes like mantı (small dumplings with spiced lamb and yogurt), ğığır (a baked meat and cheese dish), and keşkek (a traditional spiced lamb stew). Local specialties also include aş (herb porridge), cheeses made from sheep and goat milk, and honeycomb. The use of aromatic herbs and locally sourced ingredients reflects the region's pastoral traditions.


## Zonguldak

### History
Founded in the 13th century, Zonguldak became a key Ottoman coal supplier during the 16th–19th centuries. The Zonguldak Coal Railway (1885) revolutionized transport, while modernization under Atatürk reshaped the city as a regional industrial and cultural center.

### Economy
Historically a coal-mining hub, Zonguldak's economy centers on the Erdemir Steel Plant, coal exports via its major port, and recent diversification into renewable energy, though traditional industries remain dominant.

### Cuisine
Zonguldak's cuisine features Black Sea staples like grilled anchovies, smoked fish, and "kokoreç" (offal sausage), with local specialties including wild mushroom dishes and chestnut-based breads from mountainous regions.


## İzmit

### History
Historically known as Ilyon, İzmit dates back to ancient Phrygia and later became a Roman and Ottoman stronghold. It played a role in the Byzantine-Sassanid wars and was rebuilt after the 1999 earthquake. Historical sites include the 13th-century İsmet Pasha Mosque and the ruins of the ancient Laleli Church.

### Economy
İzmit serves as a major industrial and commercial hub in Turkey's Marmara region. Its economy is driven by manufacturing, automotive production, and textile industries, supported by infrastructure like the Port of İzmit. The city hosts numerous factories and logistics centers, contributing significantly to national exports.

### Cuisine
İzmit's cuisine features local specialties such as 'izmit bombası' (a sweet pastry), 'sütlü nuri' (a milk-based dessert), and 'lakerda' (smoked fish). Fresh seafood from the Sea of Marmara, including grilled anchovies and mackerel dishes, is also prominent, accompanied by traditional mezes and kebabs.


## İskenderun

### History
With roots as Alezanda in the Hittite Empire (1500 BCE), İskenderun later became Antiochia in Seleucid, Roman, and Byzantine eras, serving as a key provincial capital. The Ottomans captured it in 1517, using its port for Mediterranean trade. Historical remnants include Crusader-period fortifications and Hellenistic ruins near the modern city.

### Economy
İskenderun's economy is anchored by its strategic port, one of Turkey's top oil export hubs. It thrives on agriculture—wheat, lentils, and citrus fruits are staples—and industries like food processing, oil refining, and construction materials. Tourism also grows due to historical sites like the ancient city of Antiochia in the region.

### Cuisine
İskenderun's cuisine showcases its coastal identity with an emphasis on fresh seafood, such as grilled fish and calamari. Local specialties include 'iskender' (a spiced meat dish), meze platters with dishes like ezme and muhammara, and traditional Turkish sweets like helva. Sumac and oregano season many dishes, while ayran and rakı accompany meals.


## Manisa

### History
Manisa's history spans centuries, from its roots as the Lydian polis of 'Parasegonda' to its role in the Persian and Roman empires. It became a key Ottoman administrative center by the 15th century, renowned for its trade routes and the *Yeni Mosque*. The city hosted Atatürk during the Turkish War of Independence, solidifying its historical significance in modern Turkey's founding.

### Economy
Manisa's economy thrives on agriculture, particularly figs, hazelnuts, and apricots, which fuel a vibrant food-processing industry. The textile sector, including knitwear and fabric production, is a major employer. While coal mining in Soma historically dominated the economy, recent shifts emphasize industrial zones and renewable energy projects alongside traditional sectors.

### Cuisine
Manisa's cuisine is celebrated for its use of local ingredients like figs, apricots, and chestnuts. Traditional dishes include *Kestanehelim* (chestnut cake) and *Erek ekmeği* (rye bread), with desserts often featuring honey and nuts. Olive oil, cheeses, and preserves made from abundant orchard fruits also highlight the region's culinary heritage.


## Sakarya

### History
Sakarya dates to ancient Greek and Roman times, marked by artifacts like the 2nd-century *Uzun Köprü* (Long Bridge). It played strategic roles in the Byzantine Empire and Seljuk history. Ottoman conquest in the 14th century integrated it into Anatolia’s trade routes, while its 20th-century rail infrastructure cemented its status as a transportation and economic gateway in northwestern Turkey.

Sakarya's economy thrives on agriculture (crops like cotton, fruits, and vegetables) and food processing industries. It houses a major textile industry and a logistics hub at the Bilecik–Sakarya motorway intersection. Fresh produce from the region’s fertile lands also shapes its culinary identity.


## Eskişehir

### History
Eskişehir dates back to ancient Phrygian times and served as the Ottoman Empire's capital during its dissolution. Historic landmarks include Eskişehir Castle, the Green Tomb, and the Eskişehir Archaeological Museum.

## Erzincan

### History
Erzincan, known as Euchaita in antiquity, has been a crossroads of civilizations since the Urartian period. It was part of the Medes, Persians, and Roman/Byzantine empires before Ottoman rule. The Seljuks established a fortress here in the 12th century, and the city became a key stop on the Persian-Arab trade routes. Ottoman architecture and archaeological sites, such as Hacı Bektaş Mosque and the ruins of Kızılırmak temples, highlight its layered history.

### Economy
The economy of Erzincan centers on agriculture, particularly wheat, sunflowers, and apricot cultivation in the fertile Erzincan Basin. Livestock farming, especially sheep breeding, is prominent. The region also has mineral resources like iron and coal, while small-scale industries process agricultural goods. Tourism is growing, fueled by historical sites like the Euchaita Castle and ancient Urartian temples.

### Cuisine
Erzincan's cuisine is known for its traditional Turkish dishes, with a focus on local ingredients like apricots, grains, and dairy. Signature dishes include erişte (a syrup-sweetened wheat and noodle dish), mercimek çorbası (lentil soup), and testi koftesi (meatballs cooked in a clay pot). The region also produces soft non-yeast breads and enjoys barbecued meats, reflecting its agrarian heritage.


## Bilecik

### History
Bilecik's history dates back to the Hittite and Phrygian eras, later becoming part of the Roman and Byzantine Empires. Conquered by the Ottomans in 1295, it served as a key stop on the Silk Road and housed influential dervish lodges like the Mevlevi Dervish Lodge. The town's ancient rock tombs and Seljuk-era architecture, including the 13th-century Bilecik Castle, reflect its layered cultural heritage.

### Economy
Bilecik's economy centers on agriculture, textile production, and tourism. The region produces olives, grapes, and cereals, while its industries manufacture textiles, metalworks, and dairy products. Thermal springs like the Pamukkale thermal areas attract tourists, boosting local hospitality and crafts. The city's location near major roads between Bursa and Ankara also supports trade and logistics.

### Cuisine
Bilecik's cuisine is celebrated for its traditional Turkish dishes and local ingredients. The region is known for its mantı (spiced dough dumplings served with yogurt and garlic), testi kebabı (meat and vegetable stew cooked in an earthenware pot), and honey-nut delicacies made from forest products. Fresh vegetables, grains, and dairy from the fertile valleys contribute to hearty meals like mercimek çorbası (lentil soup) and kelle paça (tripe soup).


## Kahramanmaraş

Kahramanmaraş has inhabited since the Hittite period, later becoming part of the Roman and Byzantine Empires. It played a strategic role on trade routes like the Taurus Road. ## Düzce

Historically part of the Roman and Byzantine empires, Düzce (anciently Diospolis) has ruins dating back to the Hittite era. The city preserves Ottoman architecture and cultural heritage in landmarks like the Yıldız Hamamı.

Düzce's economy is driven by agriculture, manufacturing, and trade. Key products include textiles, dairy, and grains. The city's strategic location near the Bulgarian border boosts cross-border commerce, while tourism is growing due to historical sites like the Uzungöl and İsmail Küprülü's tomb.

Düzce's cuisine features local specialties like "Düzce kebabı" and traditional Turkish dishes made with fresh regional produce. ## Hatay

### History
Hatay has a rich history dating back to ancient times, with its capital, Antakya, once being the capital of the ancient city of Antioch. It was a significant city in the Roman and Byzantine Empires. After being under Ottoman rule for centuries, Hatay became a French mandate following World War I. It was annexed by Turkey in 1939 after a plebiscite. The region has a diverse cultural heritage influenced by Greek, Roman, Armenian, Arab, and Turkish civilizations. The historical sites, including the Antakya Castle and the Maras Earthquake, which destroyed the ancient city in AD 277, are key highlights of its storied past.

### Economy
Hatay's economy is primarily driven by agriculture, particularly the cultivation of citrus fruits, cotton, and sesame. The region's coastal position supports a significant fishing industry. Tourism is another key sector, bolstered by historical sites such as the ancient city of Antioch, the Castle of Saint Peter, and the Old Town of Alexandrette. Additionally, Hatay has industrial zones focused on food processing, textiles, and construction materials. The economy also benefits from its strategic location near the Syrian border, facilitating trade.

### Cuisine
Hatay's cuisine is a blend of Turkish and Middle Eastern influences, characterized by the use of fresh herbs, spices, and local produce. Popular dishes include 'İncirlik Soslu Pilav' (fig sauce rice), 'Lüleburgazlı Köfte' (a type of meatball), and 'Aynali Köfte'. Seafood is also prevalent due to its coastal location, with grilled fish and octopus being staples. The region is known for its unique use of sumac, a tangy red spice, and for producing high-quality citrus fruits like lemons and oranges, which are used in both savory and sweet dishes.


## Kastamonu

Fresh cheeses, honey, and mountain herbs are also highlights.


## Adıyaman

### History
Adıyaman's history dates back to the Hittite Empire (1500 BCE), making it a crossroads of civilizations. It later thrived under the Armenians as the city of Samosata, then the Romans and Byzantines. The 14th-century Adıyaman Castle stands as a key Ottoman-era fortress. Its strategic location along trade routes left behind artifacts in the city's museum, including Phrygian pottery and medieval manuscripts. The region has also influenced historical events like the Arab conquests and the Seljuk migrations.

### Economy

Amasya's economy thrives on agriculture, particularly the cultivation of hazelnuts, olives, and sunflowers, alongside cereal crops. The region also benefits from animal husbandry, producing high-quality dairy products. Tourism is growing due to historical attractions like the Amasya Castle and natural reserves. Additionally, food processing, textile industries, and artisanal crafts contribute significantly to the local economy.

### Cuisine
Amasya is renowned for its unique culinary heritage, including traditional dishes like 'Tavuk Gözü,' a rich dessert resembling halva made from semolina, and locally produced cheeses. The cuisine emphasizes farm-fresh ingredients, with staples such as ayran (a yogurt drink), sunflower oil, and seasonal vegetables. Nuts, especially walnuts and hazelnuts, are prominent in both savory and sweet preparations, showcasing the region's agricultural bounty.

## Burdur

### History
Burdur, located in southern Turkey, has a rich history dating back to the Hittite Empire around 1500 BCE. It was later influenced by the Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine periods. The city became a key settlement under the Seljuks in the 13th century and was a vital center during the Ottoman Empire, preserving its cultural heritage through ancient caravanserais and historic structures like Burdur Castle.

### Economy
Burdur's economy thrives on agriculture, livestock, and tourism. The region is known for its apricot and cherry production, as well as artisanal craftsmanship. Tourism is driven by historical sites such as Hittite rock tombs, thermal resorts, and the scenic Lake Burdur. The leather industry and food processing also contribute significantly to the local economy.

### Cuisine
Burdur's cuisine reflects traditional Turkish flavors with regional twists. Local specialties include 'Burdur kavurması' (a spiced meat and vegetable stew), lamb dishes, and walnut-based desserts. The city is also famous for 'boza' (a fermented beverage) and fresh cheeses from its mountainous regions. Street food like 'simit' (sesame bread rings) and 'börek' (puff pastry) are popular staples.


## Afyonkarahisar

### History
Afyonkarahisar, anciently named Erythrae, was established by Ionian Greeks in the 8th century BCE. It became a Roman provincial capital in 334 BCE and later flourished under the Seljuk and Ottoman Empires, leaving behind historical landmarks like the Hisar Mosque and Gölova Necropolis.

### Economy
The economy of Afyonkarahisar relies on regulated poppy cultivation for pharmaceutical use, agriculture (including hazelnuts and apricots), and tourism centered on ancient sites like the Temple of Artemis. Small-scale mining and crafts further contribute to the local economy.

### Cuisine
Afyonkarahisar’s cuisine features unique dishes like *Afyon halva* (poppy seed-based dessert), *sarımsak kebabı* (garlic kebab), and *börek* varieties. The region also produces wine from local grape varieties, often paired with traditional Turkish mezes.

## Bartın

### History
Bartın's history traces back to the Roman Empire, when it was part of the *Comum* region, known for its strategic location along critical trade routes. The area played a pivotal role in the Byzantine era, with the aqueduct now called the Bridge of the Damned (Bartın Aqueduct) standing as a testament to Roman engineering. During the Ottoman Empire, Bartın became a center for agriculture, livestock trade, and military campaigns. The region saw resistance during the Russo-Turkish Wars in the 19th century, and its cultural identity was shaped by waves of immigration and settlement. Modern Bartın became a city in 2013, preserving its historical heritage while integrating with Turkey’s evolving national identity.

### Economy
Bartın's economy is primarily driven by agriculture, livestock farming, and forest resources. The fertile soil and temperate climate support crops such as hazelnuts, which are among the region’s top agricultural exports. Forestry contributes significantly, with the surrounding natural parks providing timber and non-timber resources. Small-scale industries, including dairy production and artisanal crafts, also play a role. Tourism is gradually growing, fueled by natural attractions like the Şebinkarahisar Castle and the Bartın Bridge of the Damned, an ancient Roman aqueduct with legends tied to the Ottoman era. The Black Sea coastline supports fishing, though it is a smaller portion of the economy compared to inland activities.

### Cuisine
Bartın's cuisine is renowned for its traditional dishes rooted in rural and Black Sea culinary traditions. A staple dish is *Bartın böreği*, a rich, layered cheese bread made with dairy products from local livestock, and often served during festivals. The region also produces *kaymak* (clotted cream), *erikli helva* (apricot halva), and *yaprak döner* (grilled beef wrapped in flatbread). Local specialties often incorporate ingredients like hazelnuts, cornmeal, and wild herbs, reflecting regional agriculture. The cuisine emphasizes simplicity and the use of fresh, seasonal produce.


## Karaman

## Ardahan

### History
Ardahan, located in the northeastern part of Turkey, has a history marked by its strategic position at the crossroads of trade routes between Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Middle East. It was part of the Ottoman Empire until the early 20th century and later became a flashpoint during the Turkish-Armenian conflicts post-World War I. Archaeological findings suggest human habitation dating back to antiquity, and the region preserves medieval fortresses and churches reflecting its layered cultural heritage.

### Economy
Ardahan’s economy is primarily driven by agriculture, with a focus on apple cultivation, wheat, and livestock farming. Its mountainous terrain limits industrial development but promotes subsistence farming. Trade across the border with Armenia (often hindered by political tensions) and regional mineral resources (such as quartz and marble) contribute to economic activity. Tourism remains underdeveloped but is growing due to the region’s pristine landscapes and historical sites.

### Cuisine
Ardahan’s cuisine features hearty, rural dishes rooted in eastern Anatolian traditions. Signature items include *khash* (a slow-cooked soup of cow’s feet and tripe), *mămegâç* (a flatbread filled with cheese, leeks, and yogurt), and *sutlac* (a creamy rice pudding). Local dairy products, fermented foods, and wild herbs collected from high-altitude meadows are staples, reflecting the region’s pastoral lifestyle and adaptation to a cold climate.
